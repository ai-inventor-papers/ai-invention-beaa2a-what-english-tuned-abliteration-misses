Harm is detected, shared across languages, and orthogonal to refusal
Detection lives in a language-agnostic mid-network band.
A logistic language probe
over the residual stream falls from 99.6% at L1 to 40% at L11 and recovers to 96% by L18
(chance 17%): the middle of the network strips surface language. That same band is where
harm becomes linearly decodable, and the harm axis there is cross-lingually shared—per-
language harm axes align at 0.95 ± 0.01 Indic↔Indic and 0.90 ± 0.01 English↔Indic at L11
(Section A). English’s encoding is measurably stronger (axis norm 37.4 vs. 22–25 for any
Indic language; ∼7pp higher probe accuracy), a quantitative head-start that foreshadows
the gap.
But detection is orthogonal to the change that writes refusal.
We ask, per layer, (i)
whether refused and complied generations separate along the harm axis, and (ii) whether
the residual movement when refusing aligns with that axis (Figure 1). They dissociate. Harm
separation is a weak, middle-layer signal (Cohen d peaks at 0.95 at L9). The residual change
that accompanies refusal is near-orthogonal to the harm axis at every layer (| cos | ≤0.10,
max 0.095 at L18; per-language cosines 0.04–0.15, Section A) and its magnitude grows
monotonically into the late layers (∥∆∥: 2.2 at L4 →13.7 at L11 →81.7 at L18). So “is this
harmful?” (mid, weak, ∥ˆvharm) and “write the refusal” (late, large, ⊥ˆvharm) are different
directions in different layers (Zhao et al., 2025). The behaviour lives in a late subspace,
separate from detection—which is why uniform detection and uneven refusal can coexist.

Cross-lingual detection detail
Per-language input-position harm axes are diff-of-means with bootstrap CIs over 500 paired
resamples on the 100 (malicious, benign) twins (Arditi et al., 2024). Cross-lingual cosine
alignment at L11 (Figure 3): Indic↔Indic 0.95 ± 0.01, English↔Indic 0.90 ± 0.01. The input-
position harm axis and the output-position refusal axis are orthogonal in every language
at L11 (EN 0.04, HI 0.06, BN 0.06, PA 0.11, TA 0.15, ML 0.10) and remain so through L0–14;
weak alignment appears only at the residual-norm-outlier layer L18, replicating Zhao et al.
(2025) in a non-English MoE setting. During Indic generation the model deliberates in
English-script tokens inside <think> (ASCII fraction 0.90–0.95), and a logit lens shows the
residual stream framing its decision in English safety vocabulary (unfortunately, harmful) by
L9, consistent with Wang et al. (2026)’s universal cross-lingual refusal direction.

MoE expert routing collapses by the output
Per prompt we record a top-6 expert-usage fingerprint at each MoE layer and define each
layer’s harm-relevant expert set as the top-10 experts by |Pearson(usage, harm label)|. The
mean cross-lingual Jaccard rises through the mid-network, peaks at 0.43 at L11 (+8.7σ
above the random null 0.041), and collapses to 0.027 at L18 (Figure 5). The collapse is
harm-specific: a within-harm category signal stays at 0.117 (≈3× null) at L18. The same
peak→random shape replicates in Qwen3-30B-A3B. Concretely, English’s top-10 harm
experts at L11 overlap 5–7 of 10 with each Indic language; at L18 that drops to 0–2. The
shared upstream harm signal is realized through near-disjoint expert sets per language
by the output—a candidate mechanism for why one language’s safety routing can fail in
isolation.
