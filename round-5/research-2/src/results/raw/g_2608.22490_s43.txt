Experiments
For conducting a rigorous ablation of safety align-
ment, we need both safety-aligned models (Malign)
and their unaligned counterparts (Munalg). In our
main experiment, we instantiate Munalg using a
safety-ablated (abliterated) version of the models.
This projects the model weights orthogonal to a sin-
gle refusal direction, removing the safety defense
mechanism while leaving most of the model’s ca-
pabilities intact (Arditi et al., 2024). A detailed
consideration of this proxy is provided in Ap-
pendix A.1 and A.2. For measuring the Safety
Cost, we deliberately select the most capable model
in four widely used model families: Llama-3.3-
70B-Instruct (Grattafiori et al., 2024), Qwen2.5-
72B-Instruct (Qwen et al., 2025), gemma-3-27b-
it (Team, 2025), and Qwen3-32B (Yang et al.,
2025). We make this selection deliberately: robust
multilingual instruction-following is a prerequisite
for a meaningful utility comparison, and smaller
models exhibit known degradation in non-English
instruction-following that would contaminate the
utility signal. We therefore restrict our study to the
strongest model in each family.

A.1
Safety Ablation Method
In practice, we instantiate the unaligned coun-
terpart Munalg through weight orthogonalization
(abliteration), following the refusal-direction anal-
ysis of Arditi et al. (2024). A single refusal direc-
tion is estimated from contrastive harmful-versus-
benign prompt pairs, and the residual-stream and
MLP output weights are then projected orthogo-
nal to this direction. Because this is a closed-form
projection that involves no gradient updates and
no new training data, it removes the model’s re-
fusal behavior while leaving its base knowledge
and instruction-following ability intact. This data-
free property is exactly what lets us attribute any
measured utility gap to safety alignment rather than
to a confounding fine-tuning distribution, as we
elaborate below.

of safety alignment. In practice, raw pre-trained

to generate coherent dialogue, which introduces
severe noise into the utility comparison. Conse-
quently, the ideal proxy would be a "helpful-only"
model, a checkpoint that is trained without any
safety-triggering data while preserving the origi-

C.4
Practitioner Implications
Our findings suggest several concrete recommen-
dations for model developers. (a) Safety Cost as
a diagnostic. Conventional safety scores cannot
reveal whether a new alignment recipe quietly dam-
ages utility in some languages while leaving En-
glish untouched; Safety Cost can, making it use-
ful for catching language-specific regressions early
and for comparing recipes that look similar on
safety scores but behave very differently across
languages. (b) Report it by default. Two models
can reach near-identical refusal rates on harmful
prompts while sacrificing very different amounts
of utility, and our results show this sacrifice falls
unevenly across languages. Reporting Safety Cost
alongside refusal rates therefore gives a more hon-
est picture of cross-lingual behavior, especially for
the non-English users who bear most of the cost. (c)
Rethinking the trade-off. Much of the Safety Cost
we measure is not the unavoidable price of safety
but collateral damage from alignment recipes de-
signed primarily around English. The goal should
thus not be to choose between “more safety” and
“more utility,” but to reach a given safety level with
the cost spread evenly across languages, which is a
goal that becomes trackable only once Safety Cost
is measured.
